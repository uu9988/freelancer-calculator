Freelance Rate Guide

Learn how to set a sustainable rate, compare hourly, day, and project formats, and decide what to present to clients. This page explains the decision; for calculations, use the dedicated hourly rate tool.

What is a freelance rate?

A freelance rate is the price attached to your time, reserved capacity, or agreed deliverable. It needs to support more than the hours visible to a client. A sustainable rate also accounts for business expenses, non-billable work, time away from work, taxes, and the risk of uneven demand.

Your rate is not automatically the same as your salary converted into an hourly number. Employees are commonly paid for administration, holidays, and other work that freelancers must recover through fewer client-billable hours.

Hourly rate vs day rate vs project rate

Hourly rate

An hourly rate prices recorded client time. It is useful when the amount of work is uncertain, priorities may change, or the client wants ongoing access without a fixed deliverable.

Day rate

A day rate reserves a substantial block of your capacity. Base it on the number of hours you can realistically devote to that client, not an automatic eight-hour multiplier. Preparation and communication may reduce the delivery time available within the day.

Project rate

A project rate prices a defined scope and outcome. Your hourly or day rate can still serve as an internal labor baseline, but the final project price also needs to address direct costs, revisions, coordination, schedule risk, and scope uncertainty.

The difference between internal cost and client rate

Internal cost is what it costs your business to perform the work. It can include your delivery time, subcontractors, software, equipment allocation, insurance, and other operating costs.

Client-facing rate is what you charge. It must cover internal cost and contribute toward non-billable work, business risk, and the return required to keep the service worthwhile. Treating internal cost as the client rate leaves no room for the rest of the business.

How to estimate a sustainable rate

  1. Choose a realistic target take-home income.
  2. Add the business expenses required to operate.
  3. Account for an estimated tax rate as a planning assumption.
  4. Estimate total working time after vacation and holidays.
  5. Reduce that total to the hours that can genuinely be billed.
  6. Divide the required revenue by annual billable hours to find a minimum hourly baseline.

This baseline answers whether a rate can support your assumptions. It does not dictate the exact amount for every client or project.

Why billable hours matter

Working hours include delivery, proposals, sales, bookkeeping, communication, learning, and administration. Billable hours are the subset that can be invoiced. If you work 40 hours but invoice only 24, your billable percentage is 60%. Dividing annual revenue by all 40 hours would understate the rate needed from the hours clients actually buy.

When to use hourly pricing

  • The scope is exploratory or likely to change.
  • The client controls priorities and requests work as needed.
  • Time can be tracked and reported without excessive administration.
  • Neither party can yet define a dependable fixed deliverable.

State the rate, billing increments, reporting method, approval limits, and payment terms before work begins.

When to use project pricing

  • The deliverables and acceptance criteria are clear.
  • You can estimate effort and direct costs with reasonable confidence.
  • Revision rounds, exclusions, dependencies, and deadlines can be documented.
  • The client wants budget certainty and you can manage scope changes formally.

A project price should not be an hourly estimate with the hours hidden. It should be a considered price for a defined commitment, supported by an internal rate and cost model.

How to review and raise your rates

Review rates when costs, demand, experience, service scope, tax assumptions, or billable capacity changes. Compare realized earnings with the time and costs actually required, not only with the original estimate.

For existing clients, provide advance notice and a clear effective date. Explain the new commercial terms honestly. If budget is constrained, reduce scope, frequency, turnaround, or included support instead of silently keeping an unsustainable arrangement.

Common freelance rate mistakes

  • Converting an employee salary into an hourly rate without accounting for non-billable time.
  • Using a competitor's public rate without knowing their costs, capacity, or scope.
  • Treating every working hour as invoiceable.
  • Using the same rate for routine support and high-risk specialist work without reviewing the difference.
  • Offering discounts without receiving a useful trade-off such as reduced scope or a longer commitment.
  • Keeping the same rate after costs and responsibilities have materially changed.

Practical rate example

Suppose a freelancer has established a sustainable minimum of $60 per billable hour. They choose a client-facing hourly rate of $75 to provide room for service risk and business return.

  • Hourly engagement: $75 for each approved billable hour.
  • Reserved day: six realistic billable hours at $75 gives a $450 starting day rate.
  • Defined project: 24 estimated hours gives a $1,800 labor baseline. Adding $200 in direct costs and a 10% contingency produces a $2,200 planning price.

These figures illustrate how one sustainable hourly baseline can support different rate formats. They are not recommended market rates, and the final terms still depend on scope, risk, and client agreement.

Freelance rate FAQ

What is the difference between internal cost and a client rate?

Internal cost is what it costs your business to deliver an hour or project. A client rate must also support non-billable work, business risk, and the return you need from the engagement, so it is normally higher than internal cost.

Should I charge an hourly, day, or project rate?

Use hourly pricing when time is the clearest unit and scope may change, a day rate when the client reserves a meaningful block of your capacity, and project pricing when deliverables and boundaries are clear enough to price the complete outcome.

How do I convert an hourly rate into a day rate?

Multiply the hourly rate by the number of genuinely billable hours reserved in the day. Do not automatically assume every hour is billable; include the effect of preparation, communication, and capacity that cannot be sold elsewhere.

How often should I review my freelance rates?

Review them at least when your costs, tax assumptions, demand, experience, service scope, or available billable time changes. A regular annual review also prevents several years of cost increases from accumulating unnoticed.

How should I raise rates for existing clients?

Give reasonable notice, state the new rate and effective date clearly, and connect the change to the current scope or cost of providing the service. Do not invent a justification; offer a smaller scope if the client cannot support the new price.

What if my sustainable rate is higher than common market rates?

Recheck your assumptions first. If the rate is still necessary, consider a narrower service, stronger specialization, more efficient delivery, different clients, or project pricing. Consistently charging below a sustainable minimum does not solve the underlying gap.
