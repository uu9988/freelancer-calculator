Freelance Pricing Guide

Choose a pricing model for a freelance project, define what the price covers, and test whether the quote protects cost and profit. This guide explains the commercial decision; dedicated tools handle the calculations.

What freelance pricing means

Freelance pricing is the process of turning a defined service commitment into commercial terms a client can accept. The price depends on more than time. Scope, direct expenses, uncertainty, revisions, schedule, payment terms, and the value of the result can all affect the final quote.

A useful pricing process separates four concepts:

  • Cost is what your business expects to spend delivering the work.
  • Quote is the price offered to the client before work begins.
  • Revenue is the amount earned when the agreed work is billed and paid.
  • Profit is what remains after relevant delivery costs are deducted from revenue.

Hourly pricing

Hourly pricing charges for approved time. It works well for evolving assignments, advisory work, maintenance, and situations where the client controls changing priorities. Define the hourly rate, billing increments, reporting method, approval threshold, and what counts as billable time.

The client carries more of the scope risk because the final total changes with time used. You still need to communicate likely ranges so hourly pricing does not become an unlimited commitment.

Fixed project pricing

A fixed price covers a documented set of deliverables and conditions. Build it from estimated labor, direct project expenses, reasonable contingency, and the return needed for the project to be worthwhile.

State deliverables, exclusions, client responsibilities, revision limits, deadlines, payment milestones, and the process for additional work. Fixed pricing provides budget clarity, but unclear boundaries can transfer excessive scope risk to the freelancer.

Value-based pricing

Value-based pricing considers the importance of the result to the client rather than relying only on delivery hours. It is most credible when the desired outcome, alternatives, and business impact can be discussed openly.

It is not permission to invent a large number. A value-based proposal still needs an agreed scope, a defensible business case, and an understanding of your own cost and risk. The client must see enough value to choose the proposal voluntarily.

Retainers

A retainer reserves recurring access, capacity, or defined services for a regular payment. Specify what is included, response expectations, unused-capacity rules, overage pricing, review dates, and cancellation terms.

Do not price a retainer by multiplying a discounted hourly rate without considering the capacity you are committing. Reserved availability can prevent you from accepting other work even when the client does not use every hour.

Start with internal project cost

Before choosing a client price, estimate what delivery costs your business. Include internal labor, subcontractors, project-specific software or assets, travel, materials, and an appropriate allocation of overhead. Personal living expenses are not direct project expenses, although your wider income plan must still support them.

Internal cost is a decision floor, not the quote. Quoting at cost leaves no profit and provides no protection against estimation error.

Add scope and contingency

A quote should describe what the client receives and what assumptions support the price. Include research, meetings, production, testing, delivery, and agreed revisions when estimating labor.

Contingency is a visible planning allowance for genuine uncertainty, coordination, dependencies, and minor changes. It should not hide a weak estimate or absorb a materially different scope. Use a change order or revised quote when deliverables change.

Check expected profit

Compare the proposed revenue with internal delivery cost before sending the quote. A high price does not guarantee healthy profit if subcontractor costs, non-billable coordination, or revision time were omitted.

There is no universal profit margin that every freelancer or project should use. The acceptable result depends on your business model, risk, capacity, and alternatives. The important step is to calculate it deliberately instead of assuming the quote and profit are the same number.

Handling discounts

A discount should exchange price for something useful: reduced scope, slower delivery, simpler payment terms, a longer commitment, or a smaller revision allowance. Show the original price and discount rather than presenting the lower amount as if nothing changed.

Recheck cost and expected profit after the discount. If the price is no longer sustainable, change the scope or terms instead of relying on future work that has not been contracted.

Revisions and scope creep

Define the number and type of included revision rounds. A revision adjusts work within the agreed objective; a new deliverable, new direction, or changed requirement is additional scope.

Record requested changes, estimate their effect on time and expenses, and obtain approval for a revised price or change order before doing the extra work. Clear change handling protects the relationship as well as the budget.

Practical project pricing example

Consider a defined project expected to require 40 internal hours:

  • Internal hourly cost: $35
  • Internal labor cost: 40 × $35 = $1,400
  • Direct project expenses: $300
  • Estimated internal delivery cost: $1,700
  • Client-facing hourly basis: 40 × $75 = $3,000
  • Quote basis after adding direct expenses: $3,300
  • 10% contingency for identified uncertainty: $330
  • Quote before discount: $3,630
  • Optional 5% negotiated discount: $181.50
  • Final quote: $3,448.50

If the full amount is accepted, delivered, billed, and paid, $3,448.50 is project revenue. Compared with the simplified $1,700 delivery cost, the expected project profit is $1,748.50 before taxes and any broader costs not included in the estimate.

These figures demonstrate the relationship between cost, quote, revenue, and profit. They are example assumptions, not recommended prices, discounts, contingency levels, or profit margins.

Freelance pricing FAQ

Which pricing model works best when the scope is unclear?

Hourly pricing or a paid discovery phase is usually safer when important requirements remain unknown. A fixed price becomes more reliable after the deliverables, responsibilities, assumptions, and change process are documented.

What should a fixed project price include?

Include the labor needed for research, meetings, production, testing, delivery, and agreed revisions, plus direct project expenses and a reasonable contingency for identified uncertainty. State exclusions and payment terms separately.

Is value-based pricing just a reason to charge more?

No. Value-based pricing requires a credible understanding of the client's desired outcome and alternatives. The price still needs a clear scope and must be acceptable to both parties; value does not remove the need to understand delivery cost and risk.

How much contingency should I add to a project price?

There is no universal percentage. Use a project-specific amount based on genuine uncertainty, coordination, dependencies, and minor changes. Material scope changes should trigger a revised quote rather than being absorbed by contingency.

How should I handle a client discount?

Tie a discount to a clear trade-off such as reduced scope, a longer commitment, slower delivery, or simpler payment terms. Show the original and revised price so the concession is visible, and confirm that the reduced amount still covers cost and acceptable profit.

Is project revenue the same as profit?

No. Revenue is the amount earned from the client. Profit is what remains after the project's labor cost, direct expenses, allocated overhead, and other relevant costs are deducted. A large quote can still produce weak profit if delivery cost is underestimated.
