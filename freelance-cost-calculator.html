Freelance Cost Guide

Learn which costs to track, how project delivery costs differ from wider business expenses, and how cost connects to quotes, revenue, and profit. This guide explains the categories; dedicated tools handle the calculations.

What does “freelance cost” mean?

A freelance cost is money, time, or another business resource required to operate your business or deliver client work. Some costs belong directly to one project, while others support the business across many clients and months.

Classifying costs consistently helps you estimate projects, set prices, review profitability, and plan cash flow without counting the same outgoing twice.

Project costs vs business expenses

Project costs are tied to completing one client engagement. They include your internal labor, subcontractors, project-specific purchases, allocated overhead, payment fees, and any contingency used in the internal estimate.

Business operating expenses keep the whole freelance business running. Examples include general software, insurance, accounting, workspace, marketing, communications, equipment, and professional development.

A shared business expense can influence project cost through an overhead allocation. Record the original expense once, then use a consistent allocation method rather than entering it again as a direct purchase.

Internal labor cost

Internal labor cost represents the cost assigned to your own time. Include research, meetings, production, revisions, project administration, testing, and delivery—not only the most visible production work.

Internal labor cost = internal project hours × internal hourly cost. The internal hourly cost supports delivery planning; it is not necessarily the billable rate shown to a client.

Direct project expenses

Direct project expenses exist because a particular engagement exists. Examples include stock assets, project-only software licenses, printing, materials, travel, hosting, equipment rental, and shipping required for that client.

If the cost would continue even without that project, it is more likely a business operating expense or overhead item.

Subcontractor costs

Include payments to external designers, developers, editors, assistants, consultants, or other specialists working on the project. Record the expected payment, relevant transaction costs, and any coordination effort separately from your own internal labor.

A client may reimburse or fund the subcontractor cost, but it remains part of the internal cost of delivering the agreed scope.

Business overhead

Overhead is the portion of ongoing business costs assigned to project delivery even though those costs cannot be traced exclusively to one engagement. Insurance, accounting, workspace, administration, and general software are common examples.

You can allocate overhead using a consistent percentage, hours, revenue, or another defensible method. The goal is to prevent projects from appearing cheaper merely because shared business costs were ignored.

Software, equipment, and subscriptions

Classify software and equipment according to how they are used:

  • A general subscription used across clients is normally a business operating expense.
  • A special license purchased only for one engagement is a direct project expense.
  • Long-lived equipment used across the business should be tracked separately and allocated consistently rather than charged in full to every project.

Tax or accounting treatment depends on local rules. This guide only addresses business planning categories and does not determine deductions or depreciation.

Payment processing costs

Card processors, marketplaces, banks, and payment platforms may deduct a percentage or fixed fee from client revenue. If a fee can be traced to one payment, include it when reviewing that project's profit. General banking charges may fit better among operating expenses.

Base the fee on the amount and payment method you realistically expect, and avoid entering it both as a project cost and a general expense.

Contingency and unexpected project costs

Contingency is a cost-planning buffer for reasonable uncertainty in delivery, such as minor rework, changing dependencies, coordination, or price variation. It is separate from profit.

Contingency should not hide an undefined scope or absorb a materially different deliverable. When scope changes, document the change and revise the quote instead of relying on the buffer.

Costs that should not be mixed together

Personal expenses

Personal housing, food, transport, and other living costs are not ordinary project delivery costs. Keep them separate from business records unless you have identified a legitimate business portion under the rules that apply to you.

Taxes

Income tax is not the same as labor, subcontractor, overhead, or other delivery cost. VAT, sales tax, deductions, and income tax also follow location-specific rules and may be treated differently from revenue or expenses. Use a qualified local professional when tax treatment matters; this guide is not tax, legal, or accounting advice.

Duplicate entries

Do not record a subscription as a direct project purchase and also include the full amount through overhead. Choose a consistent classification or allocation so the same outgoing appears once in the relevant estimate.

Cost vs quote vs revenue vs profit

  • Project cost: the internal amount required to complete one project.
  • Business expense: spending required to operate the freelance business over time.
  • Quote: the price offered to the client for the agreed scope and terms.
  • Revenue: the client income earned or expected when the work is billed and paid.
  • Profit: revenue minus the relevant costs of delivering the work.

Cost is not the same as quote, and revenue is not the same as profit. A quote normally needs to exceed internal cost if the project is expected to produce profit.

Practical freelance cost example

Consider a freelance website project with these example assumptions:

  • Internal labor: 40 hours × $35 internal hourly cost = $1,400
  • Subcontractor cost: $600
  • Project-specific software and assets: $250
  • Base project delivery cost: $2,250

The $2,250 base delivery cost is still not a client quote. If the freelancer allocates 10% overhead, that adds $225. An 8% contingency applied after overhead adds $198, producing an estimated internal delivery cost of $2,673.

If a separate client quote were $3,500 and the full amount were earned, project revenue would be $3,500. Compared with the simplified $2,673 internal cost, expected project profit would be $827 before taxes, payment fees, and any other costs not included in the example.

These figures demonstrate the categories and are not recommended rates, prices, contingency levels, overhead allocations, or profit targets.

How to track freelance costs

  1. Keep project-specific purchases and subcontractors attached to the relevant client or job.
  2. Record recurring business expenses by category and review them monthly.
  3. Track your own project hours even when the client pays a fixed price.
  4. Choose one consistent method for allocating shared overhead.
  5. Reconcile estimates with actual time and spending after delivery.
  6. Keep personal expenses and tax records separate from ordinary project cost planning.

Freelance cost FAQ

What counts as a freelance project cost?

A project cost is an internal cost of completing a specific client engagement. It can include your delivery time, subcontractor payments, project-specific assets or travel, allocated overhead, payment fees, and a reasonable contingency allowance.

What is the difference between project costs and business expenses?

Project costs belong to one engagement or are allocated to it. Business expenses support the whole freelance operation, such as general software, insurance, accounting, workspace, marketing, and training. Shared expenses can be allocated as overhead without counting them twice.

Should my own time count as a project cost?

Yes. Assign an internal hourly cost to research, meetings, production, revisions, administration, testing, and delivery. This prevents your own effort from disappearing from the internal cost estimate, but the internal hourly cost is not necessarily the rate charged to the client.

Are software subscriptions project costs or business expenses?

A general subscription used across the business is normally an operating expense and may be allocated through overhead. A license or asset purchased only for one client project can be treated as a direct project expense. Use one method consistently and do not count the same cost twice.

Should subcontractor payments be included in project cost?

Yes. Include the amounts expected to be paid to external specialists for that project, together with any agreed transaction or coordination costs. Keep the subcontractor cost separate from your own internal labor so both remain visible.

What is overhead?

Overhead is the share of ongoing business costs that cannot be assigned exclusively to one project, such as insurance, accounting, workspace, administration, and general software. Allocating overhead helps prevent project delivery cost from being understated.

Is a client quote the same as project cost?

No. Project cost is an internal estimate of what delivery requires. A client quote is the price offered for the agreed scope and may also reflect profit, value, commercial risk, payment terms, and taxes or fees that apply.

What is the difference between cost and profit?

Cost is the amount required to deliver the work. Profit is the client revenue remaining after relevant project costs are deducted. Revenue is not automatically profit, and project profit is not the same as personal after-tax income.
